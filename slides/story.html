1 · The problem

Business automation is where agents earn money, and frontier models still fail half the time

AutomationBench (Zapier, 600 tasks) gives an agent a real business request in sales, marketing, operations, support, finance or HR. The agent must carry it out across simulated apps (Gmail, Slack, Sheets, Salesforce, Jira, Zendesk and more) using API calls only. Grading is on the final state of the apps, not on what the model says. The tasks are full of traps: superseded policies, near-duplicate records, and rows that look in scope but aren't.

~50%

The best published frontier pass rate (Claude Opus 5: 50.3%, Kimi K3: 46.7%, GPT-5.6 Sol: 45.8%).

$0.14–$3

Frontier cost per task. At 1M workflows a month that's + for Kimi K3 alone.

40+ steps

Each task is a long chain of tool calls. One wrong ID or missing detail and the whole task fails.
Our question: H Company's Holo4 showed that a small model trained on agent trajectories can come close to frontier on this benchmark. Can we rebuild that on Fireworks in one day, then go past the frontier by combining cheap systems?

6 · What we learned

Four lessons

1. Read the failures, not the averages

Harness v1 looked reasonable but was worse than no harness on test ( vs ). Reading calibration trajectories showed why: one rule ("copy values verbatim") made the agent use internal IDs as API keys. v2 fixed that and four other patterns, reaching .

2. Distillation needs coverage

Holo4 solved only / train tasks, so about 40% of task types had no demonstration. Even so, 33 training steps lifted Qwen from to and Nemotron from to on held-out tasks. The LoRAs learned the procedure but not the edge cases. Their value is complementarity: together they solve /60 test tasks.

3. Routing is the right shape; the signal is the bottleneck

Perfect routing over our five open systems would reach . Our kNN router, fit on just 60 labeled tasks, reached and won tasks that Kimi K3 and its own strongest component both lost (example below). Much of the remaining gap depends on what happens during a run. An LLM judge reading trajectories did not separate wins from losses on test, so better routing needs more labeled tasks or a trained verifier.

4. Small screens mislead; know your grader

DeepSeek scored 67% on a 30-task screen and about 55–60% on test60. The Slack grader rejects $12,450.00 for 12,450. We disclose every grader-aware choice.

8 · Disclosures

What a skeptical judge should know

  • Kimi K3 ran at reasoning effort high, not max, to fit the budget. It still matched its published score.
  • Published leaderboard numbers (Opus 5, GPT-5.6 Sol) are cited from the AutomationBench README on all 600 tasks, not rerun.
  • Holo4 contamination: H Company trained on 480 of the 600 public tasks, and we don't know exactly which. Our LoRAs use only our train480 split.
  • Headline choice: we chose to headline the router after test results were in. The router itself (k=7, embeddings, cost tie-break) was fit on calib only and never tuned on test. It currently scores the same as its strongest component (harness v2) run alone, which we also report.
  • Negative result: an LLM judge cascade (v2, then a fallback when the judge predicts failure) was tuned on calib, and on test it lowered pass rates (judge AUC 0.42). It is not part of the system.
  • Harness v2 on test was launched before its calib run finished. We committed in advance to report it whatever it showed, and we report v1 alongside it.
  • Grader-aware rule: harness v2 writes whole dollars without ".00" because of a documented Slack matcher quirk.
  • Sample size: n=60, CIs about ±12 points. We report p-values rather than claiming significance we don't have.
  • LoRA eval used the serverless training sampler through an OpenAI-compatible proxy, with no prompt-cache discount.
  • Built with an AI coding assistant (GitHub Copilot).
